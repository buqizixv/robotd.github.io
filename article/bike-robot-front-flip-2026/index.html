A Bicycle Robot Did a Front Flip. The Hard Part Was Landing.

Published June 5, 2026

It takes a human BMX rider years of practice to muscle a bike through a full 360-degree front flip. The margin for error is razor-thin: mistime the tuck, overshoot the rotation, land even slightly off-axis, and the result is a crash. Now a bicycle robot in Cambridge, Massachusetts, has done it for the first time without a human in the loop, without tethers, and without a ramp.

The achievement, presented at ICRA 2026 in Vienna, is more than a party trick. It is the payoff of a deceptively simple idea: that robots can learn extreme dynamic maneuvers without needing perfect demonstration data. The method, called Iterative Motion Imitation (IMI), flips the conventional imitation learning script on its head. Instead of starting with a flawless reference trajectory, it begins with one that is knowingly broken, then iteratively refines it until the robot can land on its wheels consistently.

The Robot: An Ultra-Mobility Vehicle Built for Flight

The hardware behind the feat is the Ultra-Mobility Vehicle (UMV), a custom two-wheeled robot developed at the Robotics and AI Institute (RAI) in Cambridge, Massachusetts. At first glance it looks like a child's balance bike with a mechanical skeleton bolted on top. That skeletal upper body, which the team calls "Boing," swings like a gymnast's torso to generate the angular momentum needed for aerial rotation. The UMV weighs 23.5 kilograms and is built on a carbon-fiber Specialized Hotwalk children's balance bike frame. Its five-degree-of-freedom actuation system controls the head, upper body, front fork, and rear wheel, with the head joint alone housing two permanent-magnet synchronous motors that deliver 4.5 kilowatts of peak power through planetary gearboxes and synchronous belts — enough to snap the upper body through a full rotation in under 250 milliseconds. The control stack runs at 1 kilohertz with motor-level PD loops at 8 kilohertz. This hardware allows the UMV not only to front-flip but also to jump in place to a height of one meter and leap onto a 70-centimeter platform.

The Problem Nobody Solved: Landing

Before the front flip, Shamel Fahmi — the RAI research scientist leading the UMV project — had already demonstrated jumping, balancing, and obstacle clearing on the bicycle platform. But one problem kept the team stuck: after a bicycle robot leaves the ground, how does it ensure the wheels touch down in exactly the right orientation to roll away cleanly? A front flip is not hard if you only care about rotation — a motorized pendulum can spin in the air. The hard part is landing with the wheels aligned to the direction of travel, absorbing the impact without toppling, and maintaining balance through the transition from flight to rolling contact.

In September 2024, Jeonghwan Kim joined the RAI Institute as a Ph.D. intern from Georgia Tech. His advisor, Professor Sehoon Ha, works on physics simulation and motion control. Kim himself had a strong interest in deploying reinforcement learning on real robots. Fahmi handed him the landing problem. It took Kim roughly five months to get the UMV through a complete flip and touchdown without damaging the hardware — every crash risked bending the frame or stripping a gearbox. It then took another two months to solve the post-landing balance problem: the robot would complete the flip, touch down successfully, then tip forward or backward as the suspension rebounded. Seven months of work for 0.8 seconds of flight.

Iterative Motion Imitation: Start Wrong, End Right

What elevates this work from a clever engineering hack to a publishable methodology is not the flip itself but how Kim taught the robot to do it. Conventional imitation learning is straightforward: give the robot a perfect demonstration trajectory and have it learn to reproduce that trajectory. For a front flip, however, generating a perfect demonstration trajectory is itself a fiendishly difficult optimization problem. The trajectory must respect torque limits, avoid self-collision, handle non-linear dynamics coupling between the upper body and the wheels, and terminate in a stable landing configuration. Simply computing such a trajectory from first principles can take days and often fails to converge.

Kim flipped this logic upside down. If a perfect trajectory is too hard to generate, start with one that is deliberately broken. He used a model-based controller to produce a flip reference trajectory in which the robot's upper body collides with its own structure — a physically infeasible motion by any measure. "It was unstable," Kim said. "But we used it as guidance." The process works as follows: in the NVIDIA Isaac Lab simulation environment, a reinforcement learning policy is trained with PPO to imitate the broken reference trajectory. The resulting policy rollout — itself messy and imperfect — becomes the new reference trajectory for the next training round. Crucially, the policy automatically smooths out the infeasible portions of the reference during imitation while preserving the overall motion intent. Each iteration produces a slightly more executable trajectory. After several iterations, the initially broken reference converges into a smooth, robust, physically executable flip policy that transfers zero-shot to the real UMV.

"You can have an imperfect sketch, then keep correcting it," Fahmi explained. "First execution is not going to be good. We don't care about torque or power limits as long as it does the motion. Then we have a slightly better reference, repeat, imitate again. Each iteration we can add more constraints."

The Numbers

The IMI results are stark. On the ground-level front flip task, a single-shot imitation baseline achieved a 49 percent success rate. After three IMI iterations, the success rate jumped to 93 percent. On the ground-to-platform flip — the same 360-degree rotation but landing on a 70-centimeter-high surface — the baseline success rate was zero: the single-shot approach never once landed successfully. After IMI iterations, the success rate hit 95.5 percent. Each iteration required roughly 20 minutes of simulation training. The final policy deployed onto the real UMV with no additional fine-tuning, relying only on onboard proprioception — no external motion capture, no global positioning, no visual feedback.

Beyond the Front Flip

IMI's broader significance lies in what it says about teaching robots skills that humans cannot demonstrate directly. In robotic arm manipulation, a human can teleoperate a smooth pick-and-place trajectory. But for running, jumping, flipping — extreme dynamic maneuvers where the physics are different, the timescales are too short, and the cost of failure is too high — humans cannot provide useful demonstrations. IMI offers a path: you do not need a perfect demonstration; you only need a rough shape, and iteration does the rest.

Fahmi's long-term vision is "Atlas-level mobility on bicycle agility" — a wheeled robot that can move as fast as a bicycle on flat ground while navigating obstacles and leaping onto platforms like Boston Dynamics' Atlas. That vision remains distant. The UMV still operates inside a controlled lab environment with motion capture. It has not been tested outdoors on uneven terrain or in varying lighting conditions. IMI itself is not yet an end-to-end real-time training process — each iteration still requires a round trip through simulation.

But the direction is clear. A robot bicycle that can front-flip says something that no press release can quite capture: that some obstacles are too tall for wheels to roll over but not too tall for a wheeled robot to vault. And a learning method that starts from broken trajectories and iterates toward success opens a door to extreme dynamic skills for which even perfect demonstration data cannot be generated. The 0.8 seconds shown at ICRA 2026 may be just the beginning.
