NVIDIA, Unitree, and Sharpa Launch the First Open Humanoid Robot Reference Platform

Published June 2, 2026

Jensen Huang stood on the Computex Taipei stage on June 1 and held up what looked like a routine product slide. The audience was expecting chips. What they got instead was a full humanoid robot — not a concept rendering, not a video of a prototype, but a complete reference platform that any research institution can buy, modify, and build upon. The robot is six feet tall and weighs 150 pounds. Huang, who is roughly the same height, joked: “Just like me.” Then he got serious. “Physical AI will bring the world’s largest industries into a multi-trillion-dollar opportunity,” he said. “And we are building the platform to make it happen.”

The announcement at GTC Taipei, held alongside Computex 2026, represents NVIDIA’s most ambitious move yet in robotics. The Isaac GR00T Reference Humanoid is built on the Unitree H2 Plus chassis, equipped with Sharpa Wave five-fingered tactile hands, and powered by NVIDIA’s Jetson AGX Thor T5000 AI computer running the full Isaac GR00T software stack. It is the first open reference design for a full-scale humanoid robot, and it marks a strategic shift for NVIDIA from chip supplier to platform provider.

Anatomy of the Reference Platform

The Unitree H2 Plus is a substantial piece of hardware. At 1.8 meters tall and 68 kilograms, it is built for real-world physical interaction rather than lab demonstrations. The body has 31 degrees of freedom: 6 DOF per leg, 7 per arm, 3 in the waist, and 2 in the head. The leg joints produce up to 360 Nm of peak torque, giving the robot the strength to climb stairs, squat into a working position, and recover its balance after being pushed. The arms deliver up to 120 Nm with a rated payload of 7 kilograms and a peak of 15 kilograms — enough to lift and carry objects of meaningful heft in industrial and domestic settings.

The Sharpa Wave hands are arguably the most technically impressive component of the platform. Each hand has 22 to 25 degrees of freedom across five fingers, with high-resolution tactile sensing arrays embedded in each fingertip. The sensing resolution is remarkable: over 1,000 tactile pixels per fingertip, with pressure sensitivity down to 0.02 Newtons. To put that in human terms, the robot can feel a grain of rice. Sharpa demonstrated this capability in March 2026 with a video of the Wave hands autonomously peeling an apple using both hands — the first known example of a humanoid robot performing this task without human teleoperation. The hands can also deal a pack of cards, rotate objects inside the palm, and perform the kind of fine manipulation that has historically been the hardest challenge in dexterous robotics.

Each Wave hand costs roughly $50,000, which is more than many complete robots. Sharpa began mass production in December 2025 and won a CES 2026 Innovation Award. The company was founded in late 2024 by the co-founders of Hesai Technology, the world’s largest automotive LiDAR supplier, and operates with headquarters in Singapore, R&D and manufacturing in Shanghai, and a US office in Mountain View, California.

The Brain: Jetson AGX Thor T5000

The compute platform is the Jetson AGX Thor T5000, NVIDIA’s most powerful embedded AI computer. Based on the Blackwell GPU architecture, it delivers up to 2,070 FP4 teraflops — approximately 2 quadrillion AI operations per second — with 128 GB of unified memory connected to a 14-core Arm CPU. The module consumes between 40 and 130 watts depending on workload, meaning the robot can run real-time inference for vision, natural language understanding, and whole-body motion control entirely onboard. It does not need to call the cloud. For research institutions, this changes the calculus: they can work on end-to-end embodied AI without depending on NVIDIA’s server infrastructure.

The robot carries a 15 Ah battery pack delivering roughly 1 kWh of energy, providing about three hours of operational endurance. A stereo camera array in the head covers a 140-degree horizontal field of view, and optional wrist cameras can be added for close-up manipulation. Connectivity includes Gigabit Ethernet, Wi-Fi 6, and Bluetooth 5.2.

The Full-Stack Software Pipeline

The Isaac GR00T software stack is what transforms a collection of impressive hardware components into a research platform. It covers the entire development pipeline. Isaac Teleop allows researchers to control the robot remotely and collect high-quality demonstration data. Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab provide massively parallel simulation environments where policies can be trained thousands of times faster than real time. Isaac ROS handles the middleware for deployment. And the GR00T foundation models — available on GitHub and Hugging Face — offer pre-trained vision-language-action capabilities that researchers can fine-tune for specific tasks.

This integration is the critical innovation. Humanoid robotics researchers have historically spent most of their time on system integration: getting motors to talk to cameras, cameras to talk to compute, compute to talk to the power management system. With the Isaac GR00T reference platform, all of that is pre-solved. A researcher at Stanford or ETH Zurich can unbox the robot, connect it to the software stack, and begin working on high-level AI problems in days rather than years. The reference workflow for the lower-cost Unitree G1 robot will also be released on GitHub and Hugging Face, making the platform accessible to labs with smaller budgets.

The Android Strategy for Physical AI

NVIDIA’s strategic logic is becoming clearer with each announcement. The company does not want to build and sell humanoid robots itself. It wants to be the platform upon which everyone else builds them — the Android of physical AI.

The smartphone analogy is useful for understanding the stakes. Apple’s vertically integrated iPhone model produces higher margins and a superior out-of-box experience. But Android’s open platform model, by enabling hundreds of manufacturers to compete, captured roughly 80 percent of the global smartphone market. In humanoid robotics, the same fork in the road is appearing. Tesla is building Optimus as an integrated product: the motors, the AI, the factory, the software — all controlled by one company. NVIDIA is building the substrate for everyone else: the chips, the simulation environment, the foundation models, and now the reference hardware. It is betting that the winning humanoid robot will not be designed by one company. It will be designed by many companies, all building on the same platform.

NVIDIA has been signaling this ambition since at least January 2026, when company executives described the Isaac GR00T ecosystem as a bid to become “the Android of generalist robotics” at CES 2026. The reference platform announced at Computex is the physical manifestation of that strategy. It is not a product. It is a blueprint. And NVIDIA wants everyone to use it.

Where This Fits in a Crowded Race

The humanoid robotics landscape in mid-2026 is dense with well-funded competitors. Tesla has deployed over a thousand Optimus Gen 2 units in its own factories, with plans for a Super Factory in Fremont capable of producing a million robots per year. Figure AI’s Figure 02 robots work 24-hour shifts at BMW’s Spartanburg plant in South Carolina, performing real production tasks rather than demonstrations. 1X Technologies opened a 58,000-square-foot vertically integrated factory in Hayward, California, in April and sold out its first year of NEO production in five days. And on the same day as NVIDIA’s announcement, OpenAI declared it is building its own robot hardware from scratch.

NVIDIA’s reference platform occupies a distinct niche in this landscape. It is not a consumer product or a factory robot. It is a research platform aimed at universities and research institutions, priced accordingly, and built to be modified rather than deployed at scale. But its influence may exceed its sales volume. Every PhD student who trains a manipulation policy on Isaac GR00T, every lab that publishes a paper using its sensor suite, every startup that spins out of a university project built on this hardware — they all become nodes in NVIDIA’s ecosystem. Over time, this network effect could become as defensible as CUDA’s.

The early adopters confirm this strategy. Stanford Robotics Center, ETH Zurich, UC San Diego’s ARCLab, and the Allen Institute for AI (Ai2) in Seattle have already signed on. These are not commercial customers. They are the institutions that produce the next generation of robotics talent, the papers that set research agendas, and the spin-off companies that become the next Figure AI or 1X.

The Geopolitical Context

A US semiconductor giant partnering with a Chinese robot maker on a platform destined for elite Western research institutions is not the kind of headline one expects during a technology trade war. And yet, here it is.

US-China tensions over semiconductor exports and technology transfer are at their highest point in decades. NVIDIA’s advanced GPUs are subject to strict export controls to China. The company has faced intense scrutiny from US lawmakers over its China business. Against this backdrop, announcing a deep technical partnership with a Chinese robotics company — one that some US officials have raised concerns about — is a notable risk.

NVIDIA has structured the arrangement carefully. The platform is sold as a research tool to universities, not as a general commercial product. The company is adding cybersecurity features including secure boot and confidential computing to address national security concerns. And NVIDIA executives have been explicit that the Unitree partnership is one of many: the company plans to work with robot makers in the United States, Europe, and South Korea on similar reference designs moving forward. The message is pragmatic rather than political. Chinese hardware manufacturers lead in production cost, speed, and scale, and excluding them entirely would slow down the entire industry.

For Unitree, the partnership arrives at a pivotal moment. The company’s IPO application on Shanghai’s STAR Market was approved in a record 73 days — the fastest IPO review in China in 2026. Unitree is seeking to raise approximately 4.2 billion yuan, or about $620 million, at an implied valuation of roughly 42 billion yuan ($6.2 billion). The company’s revenue grew from 159 million yuan in 2023 to 1.7 billion yuan in 2025, a compound annual growth rate of 227 percent. It shipped over 5,500 humanoid robots in 2025, more than any other manufacturer in the world. The NVIDIA partnership adds a layer of global credibility that no IPO prospectus can quantify but every investor understands.

The Long Game

The Isaac GR00T Reference Humanoid will begin shipping to research institutions in late 2026. Unitree plans to begin deliveries around October. The price for a fully equipped unit — H2 Plus chassis, two Sharpa Wave hands, Jetson AGX Thor module, full software stack — has not been disclosed, but the component costs alone suggest a figure in the range of a high-end automobile. For NVIDIA, the financial upside is not in robot sales. It is in chip sales, software subscriptions, and, most importantly, in training the next generation of robotics researchers to think within the GR00T framework.

It is too early to declare that NVIDIA’s open platform bet will replicate Android’s success. Humanoid robots are orders of magnitude more complex than smartphones, with longer development cycles, higher unit costs, and use cases that are far from standardized. But one thing is already clear. Before June 1, 2026, the humanoid robotics industry was a collection of isolated efforts — a dozen companies building a dozen incompatible systems. After June 1, they all have a place to start. In an industry where the hardest step is the first one, that might be the most valuable contribution of all.
