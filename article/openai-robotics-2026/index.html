OpenAI Enters the Humanoid Race: Sam Altman's Bet on a Robot in Every Home

Published June 1, 2026

June 1, 2026 will be remembered as the day the AI industry stopped treating the physical world as someone else's problem. Before most people had finished their Sunday coffee, two of the world's most valuable technology companies had independently signaled the same thing: software is no longer enough. Atoms matter now.

Sam Altman posted on X that OpenAI Robotics is hiring. The roles are not research fellows or policy analysts. They are full-stack hardware engineers, operations engineers, systems engineers, and machine learning engineers — the kind of job listing you write when you intend to build something that moves through rooms, picks things up, and changes the physical environment. Greg Brockman, OpenAI's co-founder, added his own note: the robotics team will be led by Aditya Ramesh, the researcher behind DALL-E and Sora, and will draw directly on the company's world simulation work. The message was unambiguous. OpenAI is no longer content to be the brain. It wants to build the body, too.

The Announcement

Altman's post was characteristically ambitious. In the short term, he wrote, OpenAI Robotics will focus on robots that support skilled workers in construction and infrastructure — electricians, plumbers, builders. The framing is practical and unglamorous, which is probably intentional. No one gets mocked for failing to build a robot that helps wire a house. The public imagination reserves its cruelty for the grander promises.

But Altman could not resist the grander promise. In the long term, he wrote, the goal is everyone having a personal robot that does anything they need. This is not a product roadmap. It is a statement of intent, and it puts OpenAI in direct competition with every company currently building humanoid robots — most notably Tesla, whose Optimus program Elon Musk has described in nearly identical language.

The salary bands tell their own story. According to the job listings, positions range from $210,000 to $310,000 annually plus equity — Bay Area market rate for top-tier hardware talent, but more importantly, a signal that OpenAI is building a real team, not a skunkworks experiment. The company has reportedly already established a robotics lab in San Francisco and is planning a second facility in Richmond, California. Data collection is underway: roughly 100 operators working in shifts, using Franka robotic arms and custom teleoperation rigs to generate training data for household tasks.

The Return

This is not OpenAI's first attempt at robotics. In 2020, the company quietly shut down its original robotics division. The team had built Dactyl, a five-fingered hand that could solve a Rubik's Cube one-handed — a genuine research achievement that required 13,000 years of simulated experience to produce roughly 20 to 60 percent success rates on its best runs. It was impressive and impractical in equal measure.

Wojciech Zaremba, the co-founder who led that effort, explained the decision with unusual candor. Robotics lacked data, he said. Language models could feast on the entire internet. A robot hand had to learn from scratch, in simulation or in a lab, and the gap between simulation and reality was punishing. The hardware was expensive. The robots broke constantly. The path to AGI through text simply looked faster. Zaremba made the call to redirect the team. It was, by his own account, the right decision for the company at the time.

What changed between 2020 and 2026? Three things. First, foundation models. The same transformer architectures that power ChatGPT have proven surprisingly adaptable to robot control — vision-language-action models can now map camera inputs and text instructions directly to motor commands. Second, simulation. NVIDIA's Omniverse and Isaac Sim can now run thousands of times faster than real time, meaning a robot can accumulate years of experience in hours. Third, economics. Humanoid robot hardware, while still expensive, has dropped far enough — and demand projections have grown large enough — that building robots no longer looks like a vanity project. It looks like a market.

Same Day, Same Message, Opposite Approach

The coincidence was almost too neat. On the same day OpenAI announced its robotics hiring spree, NVIDIA unveiled the Isaac GR00T Reference Humanoid — the first open reference design for a full-scale humanoid robot platform. Built on the Unitree H2 Plus chassis with Sharpa five-fingered tactile hands and NVIDIA's own Jetson AGX Thor computer at its core, the robot is being adopted by ETH Zurich, Stanford, and UC San Diego for academic research. It is not a product NVIDIA will sell. It is a blueprint NVIDIA wants everyone else to use.

The contrast between the two announcements reveals two fundamentally different theories about how AI companies should enter the physical world. OpenAI is going vertical: hire hardware engineers, build robots in-house, control the stack from foundation model to fingertip. NVIDIA is going horizontal: provide the chips, the simulation software, the foundation models, and now the reference hardware design, then let a thousand manufacturers compete on price and form factor.

Both approaches have analogues in the smartphone industry. OpenAI looks like Apple — integrated, controlled, premium. NVIDIA looks like Google with Android — open(ish), widely adopted, making money on the infrastructure layer regardless of which hardware brand wins. The smartphone analogy is imperfect, but the strategic logic is the same. In a new hardware category, you either own the product or you own the platform. Nobody owns both.

A Crowded Field

The humanoid robotics landscape in mid-2026 is already dense with well-funded competitors, and OpenAI's entry makes it denser. Tesla has over a thousand Optimus Gen 2 units deployed in its own factories, with a target price of $20,000 to $30,000 and a planned Super Factory in Fremont capable of producing a million robots per year. Figure AI has Figure 02 robots working 24-hour shifts at BMW's Spartanburg plant — real production work, not demos — and has raised enough capital to value the company well into the billions. 1X Technologies opened a 58,000-square-foot vertically integrated factory in Hayward, California in April 2026, began mass-producing its NEO humanoid at $20,000 per unit, and sold out its first year of production in five days.

Then there are the software players. Physical Intelligence, the San Francisco startup backed by over a billion dollars in funding, released its pi-zero-point-seven foundation model in April 2026 — a vision-language-action system that demonstrated emergent compositional generalization, meaning it could combine skills learned from different tasks to solve entirely new ones without additional training. Google DeepMind continues to develop its RT series of robotics transformers. And NVIDIA's GR00T platform now counts 110 robot brain developers in its ecosystem, a number that has nearly quadrupled in a year.

OpenAI's position in this landscape is uniquely complicated. The company holds minority stakes in both Figure AI and 1X Technologies through its startup fund. It previously had a formal partnership with Figure that ended when OpenAI decided to pursue internal robotics development. The relationships are now somewhere between awkward and adversarial — former allies who saw the same future and decided they wanted to own it themselves.

What Changed, and Why Now

The simplest explanation for why 2026 looks different from 2020 is that the enabling technologies converged. Foundation models crossed a performance threshold where they could reliably interpret visual scenes and generate motor commands. Simulation environments became fast enough to train policies at scale. Hardware costs dropped. But there is a less technical reason too, and it may matter more.

The language model market is maturing. Competition in LLMs has become a brutal, capital-intensive slog where differentiation is shrinking and margins are compressing. Every major AI lab is looking for the next vector of growth. For OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and increasingly NVIDIA, the answer is the same: move beyond the screen. The physical world — manufacturing, logistics, construction, home services — represents a total addressable market measured in the tens of trillions of dollars. Jensen Huang put the number at $40 trillion at GTC 2026. Even if that figure is aspirational, the reality is that physical automation is the largest unconquered market in the global economy.

Goldman Sachs projects the humanoid robot market alone will reach $38 billion by 2035. Morgan Stanley sees $1.2 trillion by 2040. These are the kinds of numbers that make boardrooms pay attention, and they explain why OpenAI — a company that five years ago decided robotics was too data-poor and hardware-intensive to pursue — is now hiring hardware engineers as fast as it can find them.

Software-First vs Hardware-First

The deepest question raised by June 1 is not who will build the best robot. It is which approach to building robots will win. Tesla's strategy is hardware-first: design a manufacturable humanoid, produce it at automotive scale, use the resulting fleet to generate training data, and improve the software iteratively. The logic is that software eventually becomes commoditized, and the durable advantage lies in the ability to manufacture millions of units at low cost.

OpenAI's strategy, at least as implied by Altman's announcement, is software-first: build world-class foundation models that understand the physical world, collect high-quality training data through carefully instrumented teleoperation, and use software superiority to overcome the hardware learning curve. The logic is that hardware eventually becomes commoditized, and the durable advantage lies in the intelligence that animates it.

Both arguments are plausible. Both have been proven right and wrong in different industries at different times. The smartphone market rewarded Apple's integrated approach and punished everyone who tried to compete on hardware alone. The PC market rewarded Microsoft's platform approach and commoditized the box makers. Which pattern applies to humanoid robots is the multi-trillion-dollar question that 2026 will begin to answer.

What is already clear is that the AI industry's relationship with the physical world has fundamentally changed. For years, the most advanced AI systems in existence operated entirely behind glass — answering questions, generating images, writing code, but never touching anything. June 1, 2026 marks the moment when the two most important companies in AI both decided, independently and simultaneously, that staying behind glass was no longer enough. The robots are coming. The people building them just got a lot more formidable.
