The Robot That Thinks Before It Acts: How NeoVerse-ABot Solved Action Hallucination at ICRA 2026

Published June 9, 2026

On June 5, 2026, at the IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation (ICRA 2026) in Vienna, a team from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) quietly solved one of the most stubborn problems in robot learning: the hallucination problem. Their model, NeoVerse-ABot, scored 0.829 on the World Model track of the AGIBOT WORLD CHALLENGE — first place out of 526 teams from 27 countries — and in doing so, demonstrated something the field has been chasing for years: a robot that can accurately imagine the consequences of its actions before it acts.

The term 'action hallucination' sounds technical, but the problem is intuitive. When a robot attempts to grasp an object, its internal world model might visualize a successful grasp — fingers closing around the target — even though the robot's hand actually closed on empty air. The model literally hallucinated a successful outcome. This is not a corner case; it is a systematic flaw in how most world models are trained. They learn from successful demonstrations, so they learn to predict success, whether or not the physics supports it. NeoVerse-ABot broke that pattern.

What Is a World Model, and Why Does It Matter?

A world model is a neural network that learns the physics of an environment — how objects move, how forces propagate, how actions lead to outcomes. Think of it as the robot's internal simulation engine. When a robot considers picking up a cup, the world model runs a mental simulation: 'If I move my gripper to these coordinates and close with this force, the cup will follow this trajectory.' A good world model catches failures before they happen. A hallucinating world model shows the cup being picked up perfectly — even when the gripper is six inches to the left.

The implications extend far beyond grasping. World models are the foundation of the next generation of robotics AI. NVIDIA's Cosmos 3, released just days before ICRA, is explicitly a world foundation model. DeepMind's Genie 2 generates interactive environments from single images. The race to build accurate, physically-grounded world models is arguably the most important competition in robotics AI right now — because the model that can accurately simulate physics in its 'imagination' can plan, reason, and learn without needing millions of costly real-world trials.

This is where NeoVerse-ABot enters the story. Its success at ICRA 2026 is not just a competition result. It is a proof point that the action hallucination problem — which many in the field believed was inherent to learned world models — can be systematically overcome.

The Three Innovations Behind NeoVerse-ABot

The NeoVerse-ABot team, a collaboration between the CAS Institute of Automation (Pattern Recognition Lab) and Gaode CV Lab, introduced three innovations that collectively eliminated action hallucination.

First, they made failure a first-class training signal. Standard world model training uses datasets of successful robot executions — pick-and-place, insertion, stacking — and trains the model to predict the visual outcomes of those successful actions. The problem is that the model never learns what failure looks like, so it defaults to predicting success regardless of the input. The NeoVerse-ABot team deliberately mined failure trajectories: grasps that closed on air, objects that slipped during lifting, parts that misaligned during insertion. These failure clips were weighted more heavily in the training loss than successful ones, forcing the model to learn the boundary between success and failure rather than just the positive examples.

Second, they introduced a reinforcement learning feedback loop that evaluates the physical plausibility of generated outcomes. After the world model generates a predicted visual sequence for a given action, a separate RL-based critic scores it on three criteria: action consistency (does the predicted motion match the commanded action?), causal validity (do effects follow causes in a physically reasonable order?), and physical compliance (do objects move in ways consistent with mass, friction, and gravity?). This critic is not trained on human labels — it learns physics from first principles through trial and error in simulation, then transfers that knowledge to evaluate the world model's outputs. The critic's scores are fed back into the world model as additional training signal, creating a self-improving loop.

Third, and most fundamentally, they built what the team calls 'physical imagination' — a latent-space reasoning mechanism that allows the model to simulate multiple possible outcomes before committing to a prediction. Rather than generating a single deterministic future frame, NeoVerse-ABot samples from a distribution of possible futures and selects the most physically consistent one. This is conceptually similar to how a human might visualize reaching for a cup: you implicitly simulate several possible trajectories and pick the one that feels right. The robot does the same, but explicitly and quantifiably.

The results speak for themselves. NeoVerse-ABot scored 0.829 overall, ranking first in both the image quality and action following sub-metrics. The team's CAS stablemates, PAI@IAII, took second place. China's University of Science and Technology (USTC) Loop team placed third. Chinese teams swept the podium — a result that was noted extensively in the conference's closing remarks.

The Challenge: 526 Teams, Two Tracks, Real Robots

The AGIBOT WORLD CHALLENGE 2026, hosted by Shanghai-based humanoid company Zhiyuan (Agibot), was structured as two parallel tracks: 'World Model' and 'Reasoning to Action.' Both tracks used a unified evaluation framework with standardized online benchmarks followed by real-world validation on Zhiyuan's G2 humanoid robot. This online-to-real pipeline was itself a technical achievement: teams submitted their models for remote evaluation on standardized test sets, and the top performers advanced to live testing on physical hardware at ICRA 2026 in Vienna.

The World Model track, which NeoVerse-ABot won, required models to predict the visual outcomes of specified action sequences on physical robots. The evaluation measured two things: image quality (how realistic and detailed the predicted frames were) and action following (how accurately the predicted outcome matched the actual commanded action). A model that generates beautiful, photorealistic images of a robot performing a task — but shows the robot succeeding at a task it actually failed — would score high on image quality but zero on action following. NeoVerse-ABot was the only model in the competition that scored above 0.8 on both metrics simultaneously.

The Reasoning to Action track, won independently by a separate team, evaluated end-to-end task completion rather than world prediction. The parallel structure was deliberate: the organizers wanted to advance both the 'imagination' capability (world models) and the 'execution' capability (reasoning to action) simultaneously, recognizing that embodied intelligence requires both.

Why This Matters for the Robotics Industry

The action hallucination problem has been a quiet crisis in robot learning. As world models have grown larger and more capable — NVIDIA's Cosmos, Google DeepMind's Genie 2, Skild's Brain 1.0 — the hallucination problem has become the primary obstacle to deploying these models in safety-critical applications. A warehouse robot whose internal simulation predicts a perfect pick every time, while its actual pick success rate hovers at 60%, is not just inaccurate; it is dangerous. It will attempt picks that cannot succeed, potentially damaging objects or jamming production lines.

NeoVerse-ABot's approach suggests a general solution. By training on failure, evaluating with a physics-based critic, and sampling across multiple possible futures, the model learns the actual boundary of the physically possible rather than a biased sample of the successful. This approach is architecture-agnostic: the same techniques could be applied to NVIDIA's Cosmos, DeepMind's Genie, or any other world model architecture. The team has indicated they plan to open-source the training framework, which would accelerate adoption across the field.

The broader significance is architectural. The dominant paradigm in robotics AI has been 'sense-plan-act' — perceive the environment, plan a trajectory, execute it. World models promise a fundamentally different paradigm: 'imagine-evaluate-act' — simulate possible futures, evaluate their outcomes, act on the best one. NeoVerse-ABot demonstrates that this paradigm is achievable, provided the world model is trained to be honest about what it does not know.

The China Robotics AI Landscape

NeoVerse-ABot's win is part of a broader pattern. Chinese teams and companies have been quietly building world-class robotics AI capabilities. Zhiyuan (Agibot), the challenge host, is one of China's leading humanoid robot companies — its G2 robot was the standard hardware platform for the challenge's real-world validation. Unitree Robotics, which partnered with NVIDIA on the Isaac GR00T reference humanoid announced at Computex 2026 just days earlier, represents the hardware side of the same ecosystem. The AGIBOT challenge itself, with 526 teams from 27 countries, demonstrated that China is not just participating in the global robotics AI race but increasingly setting the agenda.

The contrast with the dominant narrative from American and European companies is instructive. While NVIDIA, Google DeepMind, and Skild AI push forward with ever-larger foundation models trained on ever-larger compute clusters, the NeoVerse-ABot team achieved a breakthrough that addresses a fundamental limitation of those large models — and did it with a focused academic-industrial collaboration rather than a billion-dollar compute budget. This suggests that the next phase of robotics AI progress may be less about raw scale and more about training methodology, data curation, and architectural innovations that make models smarter rather than bigger.

For Robot D readers, the key takeaway is this: June 2026 has already been a landmark month for robotics AI, with NVIDIA's Cosmos 3, Skild Brain 1.0, and now NeoVerse-ABot's world model breakthrough all landing within a single week. The convergence of large-scale foundation models with physically-grounded world models — models that can accurately imagine the consequences of their actions — may be the technical foundation on which the next generation of capable, safe, and reliable robots is built. The fact that the world model breakthrough came from a Chinese academic team rather than a Silicon Valley giant only underscores how global this race has become.
