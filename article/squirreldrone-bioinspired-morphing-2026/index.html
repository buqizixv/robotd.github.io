The Squirrel That Taught a Drone to Fly: TU Delft Abandons Bird Logic for a Whole-Body Morphing Breakthrough

Published June 10, 2026

For decades, drone engineers have been staring at birds. Morphing wings, flapping mechanisms, feather-inspired control surfaces — the entire field of bio-inspired aerial robotics has essentially been an extended homage to avian flight. It worked well enough. But it also locked the industry into a single evolutionary lineage, as if birds were the only creatures that ever figured out how to move through air. They are not.

On June 4, 2026, a team at Delft University of Technology published a paper in Nature Communications that politely but firmly dismantles this assumption. Their creation — the SquirrelDrone — doesn't fly like a bird. It flies like a flying squirrel. And according to their wind tunnel data and outdoor flight tests, it does things no bird-inspired drone has pulled off.

The Problem With Birds

Here's what bird-inspired drones get wrong: birds control flight primarily through their wings. They change wing sweep, adjust camber, twist feathers. The rest of the body — the head, the legs, the tail — assists but doesn't fundamentally reconfigure the aerodynamic shape. This works beautifully in open skies. In cluttered environments — dense forests, narrow canyons, urban corridors — it starts showing cracks. A bird's body is always a bird's body. It can't become something else mid-flight.

A flying squirrel can. Watch one glide through a forest at night and you'll see it stretch its limbs, flatten its body, arch its spine, deploy its tail as a rudder, and let its loose skin membrane — the patagium — billow into a living wing that continuously changes shape. Every part of the animal participates in flight control. The whole body is the aerodynamic surface. This isn't just a different flight mechanism; it's a different philosophy of how an airborne creature relates to the air around it.

From Squirrel to Schematic

The TU Delft team — PhD candidate Liming Zheng, Assistant Professor Sander van Zuijlen, and Associate Professor Salua Hamaza of the BioMorphic Intelligence Lab — translated three specific biological mechanisms into hardware:

First: coordinated forelimb and hindlimb motion. In flight, the drone's four limbs move independently, continuously reshaping the aerodynamic body. Unlike a fixed-wing aircraft where the wing is a permanent structure, the SquirrelDrone's "wing" is the ever-changing distance and angle between its limbs.

Second: spine and tail morphing. A flexible central spine allows the drone to bend and twist its body mid-flight, while an actuated tail provides active directional control. Think of a cat twisting in freefall — the spine does most of the work.

Third: a soft passive membrane. This is the patagium equivalent — a flexible membrane stretched between the limbs that naturally deforms under airflow, passively enhancing lift and drag as needed. No motors, no servos. The air itself shapes it.

These three mechanisms together create something genuinely new: a flying robot whose entire body is a control surface. Not wings with control surfaces attached. The body is the wing.

Four Prototypes, One Problem

The team built four prototype versions before arriving at a working system. This wasn't a clean, linear development. Zheng described the process bluntly: "Because the drone changes its entire body shape during flight, we could not evaluate it like a conventional fixed-wing aircraft." Every time they modified one morphing parameter, the entire aerodynamic profile shifted. Wind tunnel experiments had to be designed from scratch. Outdoor flight tests required custom instrumentation.

The results, when they finally came, were unambiguous. Whole-body morphing delivered measurable improvements across three axes:

Agility — the ability to rapidly rotate and reorient. The SquirrelDrone could execute rapid directional changes that would stall a rigid platform. The coordinated limb extension and retraction effectively shifted its center of mass and aerodynamic center simultaneously, allowing rotational accelerations that fixed-geometry designs simply cannot match.

Maneuverability — the ability to follow curved trajectories. Sharper turns, steeper pull-up trajectories, tighter loops. When the limbs and spine work together, the drone can carve through air along paths that look almost biologically organic rather than mechanically constrained.

Stability — the counterintuitive one. Adding more moving parts usually makes a system less stable. But the passive membrane's natural deformation under airflow acted as a self-correcting mechanism. Roll disturbances were damped by the membrane's passive response. Tail and body morphing provided additional stabilization channels. The drone was more stable because it was more deformable.

Why This Matters Beyond the Lab

The immediate reaction to any bio-inspired drone paper is skepticism about real-world relevance. Lab demonstrations, nature metaphors, pretty pictures — the field has earned its share of cynicism. But the SquirrelDrone's implications are concrete.

Drones that can morph their entire body shape don't just fly differently — they can access environments that rigid drones cannot. A dense forest canopy is navigable if your drone can flatten itself to slip through gaps, then expand its control surface for stable flight in an open clearing. Urban search and rescue, where drones must navigate collapsed structures with irregular openings, becomes feasible. Agricultural inspection, where the drone must transition between open fields and tight greenhouse corridors, gets a single-platform solution.

There's also a manufacturing angle. Soft morphing structures, by their nature, absorb impact better than rigid airframes. A drone that can deform passively on collision — and keep flying — has fundamentally different safety and durability characteristics. For applications near people, this matters enormously.

The Paradigm Shift Nobody Announced

The SquirrelDrone represents something larger than a single paper or a single prototype. It's a challenge to a forty-year assumption in aerial robotics: that aircraft should be rigid with discrete control surfaces. Flying squirrels never got that memo. They've been doing whole-body aerodynamics for millions of years while engineers optimized wings as if the rest of the body were dead weight.

Hamaza put it simply: "Gliding mammals achieve flight control differently. They morph their entire body as an integrated aerodynamic system." The word "integrated" is doing heavy lifting here. In conventional aircraft design, lift generation, stability, and control are handled by separate subsystems. In the SquirrelDrone, they're all properties of the same morphing body. That's not an incremental improvement. It's a category change.

The paper doesn't claim the SquirrelDrone is ready for commercial deployment. It doesn't need to. What it demonstrates is a design principle — whole-body morphing — that opens a design space most drone engineers didn't know existed. The next generation of aerial robots may look less like airplanes and more like animals that never had the luxury of rigid wings.
