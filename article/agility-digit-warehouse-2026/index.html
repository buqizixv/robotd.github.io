Agility's Digit Is the Only Humanoid Robot Actually Making Money in Warehouses Right Now

Published June 1, 2026

In February 2026, Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada did something no other automaker had done before: it signed a commercial contract to put humanoid robots on the payroll. Not a pilot. Not a research partnership. Seven Agility Digit robots, leased under a Robots-as-a-Service agreement, now unload empty auto-parts totes from automated tuggers and reload full ones at the company's Woodstock, Ontario plant — the same factory that churns out RAV4s and RAV4 Hybrids by the thousands.

The deal is modest in scale — seven robots, one factory floor — but its symbolism lands harder than the numbers suggest. After a year-long pilot with three Digit units that proved the robots could handle real production cycles without breaking things or themselves, Toyota decided to pay for more. Real money changed hands. This is not a demo reel.

And it is not an isolated case. Agility Robotics, the Oregon-based company behind Digit, has been quietly assembling the industry's most credible track record of humanoid deployment in logistics environments. While the rest of the humanoid sector churns out splashy videos and carefully staged press events, Digit robots have been clocking actual shifts.

How Digit Got Here

Agility Robotics was founded in 2015, spun out of the Dynamic Robotics Laboratory at Oregon State University. The company's intellectual DNA traces back to Jonathan Hurst, a professor who spent years studying how birds and animals walk — specifically how their legs store and release energy during gait cycles. That biomechanics research became the foundation for Digit's most distinctive physical feature: backward-bending, ostrich-like legs.

These are not a design gimmick. Traditional humanoid legs with forward-bending knees require the robot to step wide or twist awkwardly when reaching for objects at ground level. Digit's reverse-knee configuration lets it squat straight down between its own feet, reaching shelves near the floor and stacking totes up to nearly six feet high — all without the hip-joint gymnastics that eat battery life and introduce points of mechanical failure. It is a deliberately unsexy solution to a specific problem: warehouses are full of shelves at awkward heights, and fixed automation cannot handle the variability.

Digit stands 175 centimeters tall and weighs about 64 kilograms. Its payload capacity is rated at 16 kilograms sustained, though the company has stated that the upcoming Gen 5 version, slated for broader rollout in 2027, will handle 50 pounds (roughly 22.7 kilograms) — intentionally aligned with OSHA's recommended manual lifting limits. Battery runtime is the robot's strongest card: up to eight hours on a single charge, nearly double what competitors like Tesla's Optimus Gen 3 and Figure 03 currently claim. In practical deployments, shifts run about 7.5 hours, with Digit handling roughly 97% of assigned tasks successfully in live production environments.

On the perception side, Digit carries lidar for 3D mapping, four Intel RealSense depth cameras, and an inertial measurement unit. Compute runs on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, delivering roughly 275 TFLOPS. The software stack is managed through Agility Arc, a cloud-based fleet management platform that handles task assignment, battery monitoring, and layout updates across multiple robots simultaneously. Notably, CEO Peggy Johnson has described the company's AI approach as “LLM-agnostic” — Digit can accept verbal instructions through Claude, Gemini, or other large language models, but the core control stack does not depend on any single AI provider.

The 100,000-Tote Proof Point

In November 2025, Agility announced that Digit had moved over 100,000 totes at a GXO Logistics facility in Flowery Branch, Georgia. The announcement came during live production cycles, not a controlled test environment. Digit was moving inventory from autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) to conveyors, stacking totes onto different floor locations, and working continuous shifts alongside human employees.

The timing of the announcement was not accidental. It landed one day after competitor Figure disclosed that its F.02 fleet had processed roughly 90,000 sheet-metal parts at BMW's Spartanburg plant. The back-to-back reveals marked a broader industry pivot: the metric that matters is no longer “can the robot do the task?” but “how many cycles can it complete before something goes wrong?”

GXO, the world's largest pure-play contract logistics provider, operates roughly 970 facilities across 27 countries. Its interest in humanoid robotics is straightforward. Warehouse labor turnover in the United States runs at roughly 40 to 50 percent annually for entry-level roles, according to industry data. The work is repetitive, physically demanding, and increasingly difficult to staff. A robot that can handle tote movement — the unglamorous middle-mile work between AMRs, conveyors, and shelving — addresses a persistent bottleneck that fixed automation has never fully solved.

The economics of Digit's GXO deployment are worth examining closely. Agility offers Digit through a Robots-as-a-Service model at approximately $25 per hour of operation. The fully loaded cost of a U.S. warehouse worker — wages, benefits, insurance, training, and injury risk — runs around $30 per hour or higher in major logistics hubs. Digit undercuts that on day one. And Peggy Johnson has stated publicly that the company's target is to drive operating costs down to $2 to $3 per hour at scale. At that price point, the ROI case becomes difficult for any logistics operator to ignore.

Toyota, Schaeffler, and the RaaS Model

The Toyota Canada deal, announced in February 2026, represents a structural shift in how industrial buyers approach humanoid robotics. Toyota did not purchase seven Digit units outright (each currently priced around $250,000 for pilot deployments). Instead, the automaker subscribed to a RaaS agreement that converts what would be a major capital expenditure into a flexible operational expense. Agility handles maintenance, software updates, and hardware refreshes. If a next-generation Digit arrives, Toyota can swap units without writing off its initial investment.

This model echoes what happened in enterprise software two decades ago, when SaaS replaced perpetual licenses. Incumbent industrial automation vendors — the Fanucs and ABBs of the world — still predominantly sell hardware as capital equipment. Agility's subscription approach lowers the barrier to entry and builds recurring revenue, which investors tend to value at higher multiples than one-time equipment sales.

Schaeffler, the German motion technology conglomerate with deep ties to the automotive supply chain, is running a separate Digit pilot at an auto-parts plant in South Carolina. Schaeffler has also made a strategic equity investment in Agility and has publicly discussed plans to deploy humanoid robots across 100 factories globally by 2030. Separately, Amazon — an early Agility investor through its Industrial Innovation Fund — continues to test Digit in its fulfillment network, and Latin American e-commerce giant Mercado Libre began deploying Digit at a San Antonio, Texas fulfillment center in late 2025.

RoboFab and the Production Question

Agility's manufacturing hub is RoboFab, a 70,000-square-foot facility in Salem, Oregon. The plant was purpose-built for humanoid robot assembly and uses a modular workstation architecture that can scale production as demand grows. Agility claims peak annual capacity of 10,000 units.

The reality, as of mid-2026, is more measured. Actual commercially deployed Digit units are still measured in the hundreds, not thousands. The bottleneck is not production capability but safety certification. Currently, Digit operates inside protective cages or designated work cells when deployed alongside humans — a constraint that limits where and how the robot can be used. A Digit unloading totes behind a Plexiglas barrier is useful, but a Digit that can walk freely across a loading dock, through aisles, and into storage areas is transformational.

That is the next milestone. At the Abundance Summit in early 2026, Peggy Johnson announced that Agility will deliver the first “cooperatively safe” humanoid robot by late 2026 — a version of Digit certified to operate without protective barriers. The mechanism, as Johnson described it, is bluntly practical: if a human approaches too closely, the robot detects the intrusion and brings itself to the ground, ensuring no harm is done. It is not elegant. It is, however, a solvable engineering problem that unlocks vastly more deployment scenarios than a caged robot can address.

The Competitive Picture

Agility's position in the humanoid landscape is unusual. The company does not have the raw technical firepower of Figure, whose Figure 03 boasts 48-plus degrees of freedom with palm-mounted cameras and tactile sensors capable of detecting three grams of force. It does not have the manufacturing scale of Tesla, whose Fremont factory is theoretically tooled for up to one million Optimus units per year. It does not have the consumer-facing ambition of 1X Technologies, whose $20,000 NEO robot began early user shipments in the U.S. in 2026 as a home companion device.

What Agility has is focus. Digit does one thing — tote handling in logistics environments — and it does it with enough reliability that customers are paying real money for it, not for a pilot or a proof-of-concept. Figure 03, for all its technical brilliance, has not yet deployed commercially in a warehouse. Tesla's Optimus Gen 3, now in limited production at the Fremont factory, is being tested internally and will not be sold to external customers until late 2027 at the earliest, according to Tesla's own timeline. 1X NEO is deliberately designed for homes, not fulfillment centers — it weighs just 30 kilograms and uses soft actuators for safety around people, which also limits its industrial utility.

Apptronik, another U.S.-based competitor, is pursuing a similar RaaS warehouse model with its Apollo robot, and has partnered with GXO as well. But Digit's eight-hour battery life and its specific design optimization for tote-height ergonomics give Agility a meaningful head start in this narrow but high-volume niche.

What This Tells Us About the Humanoid Market

The humanoid robotics industry is absorbing a lesson that the autonomous vehicle industry learned painfully over the past decade: general-purpose capability is exponentially harder than vertical-specific deployment. A robot that can fold laundry, cook dinner, and walk the dog is a magnificent research goal. A robot that can reliably move totes from Point A to Point B for seven hours straight is a business.

Agility's trajectory suggests that the first wave of commercially viable humanoid robots will not be generalists. They will be narrow, task-specific machines optimized for environments where the economics of labor are most strained and the variability of the work is low enough that today's AI can handle it. Warehouses fit that description. So do factory floors, distribution centers, and eventually construction sites and last-mile delivery hubs.

The question hanging over Agility is whether this focused strategy can survive the arrival of Tesla's manufacturing juggernaut. If Optimus Gen 3 actually reaches the market at $25,000 to $30,000 per unit — less than one-eighth of Digit's current pilot pricing — the economic equation shifts dramatically. Agility's counterargument, implicit in every deployment milestone it publicizes, is that real-world operational data matters more than spec sheets. A $30,000 robot that fails 20 percent of its tasks or requires constant human supervision is not cheaper than a $250,000 robot that works.

Agility's next act is clear enough. Secure the cooperatively safe certification. Ship Gen 5 Digit with its 50-pound payload capacity. Raise fresh capital later in 2026 to expand RoboFab production. And continue converting the operational data from GXO, Toyota, Schaeffler, and Amazon into a defensible moat that no amount of venture funding can replicate overnight. Ten thousand robots per year from a single factory in Oregon is not a Tesla-scale number. But for a company that has already proven its machines can pay for themselves, it may be exactly the right number.
