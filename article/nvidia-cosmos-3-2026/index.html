The Omnimodel Is Here: Inside NVIDIA Cosmos 3, the Open Foundation for Physical AI

Published June 4, 2026

Jensen Huang has never been shy about making predictions, but at GTC Taipei on June 1, he made one that deserves close attention. Standing on stage at COMPUTEX 2026, he said the words: "The big bang of physical AI is just around the corner." Then he unveiled the catalyst — NVIDIA Cosmos 3, the first fully open-source "omnimodel" for Physical AI, released under permissive licenses on Hugging Face and GitHub.

This is not another large language model with a robotics wrapper. Cosmos 3 is something the industry has talked about for years but no one has actually built: a single foundation model that natively understands and generates text, images, video, ambient sound, and — critically — action trajectories. One model, one architecture, one training run. Developers can use it as a vision-language model, a world simulator, a policy backbone for robots, or all three at once.

The ambition is enormous. The implications for robotics, autonomous vehicles, and industrial automation are immediate. And unlike most frontier AI models, Cosmos 3 is not locked behind an API paywall. It is open. The weights are downloadable. The code is on GitHub. Any researcher, any startup, any university lab can build on it.

Two Towers, One Model

The technical foundation of Cosmos 3 is a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture that splits the model into two specialized "towers" sharing a common base. The Reasoner tower is an autoregressive vision-language model that interprets scenes, objects, motion, and physical context. The Generator tower is a diffusion transformer that produces physics-grounded video, audio, and action sequences conditioned on the Reasoner's understanding.

This dual-tower design is not a gimmick. It addresses a fundamental tension in Physical AI: reasoning about the world requires understanding what happened and what might happen next, but generating physically plausible predictions requires a different kind of computation — one that can model continuous high-dimensional distributions like video frames and joint trajectories. By separating the two functions while keeping them in a unified model, Cosmos 3 achieves something no previous open model has: it can both think before it acts and generate what that action would look like, sound like, and feel like in the physical world.

The training data is commensurate with the ambition. NVIDIA trained Cosmos 3 on 20 trillion tokens spanning roughly 1 billion images, 400 million real and synthetic videos, environmental audio recordings, text corpora, and human and robot action trajectories. The model supports multiple resolution tiers from 256p to 720p, variable aspect ratios, frame rates from 10 to 30 FPS, and output sequences from 5 to 300 frames. Quantization support for FP8 and the newly introduced NVFP4 4-bit format means it can run efficiently on workstation GPUs as well as datacenter clusters.

Three Flavors for Three Use Cases

Cosmos 3 ships in two variants immediately, with a third on the way. Cosmos 3 Super, at 64 billion parameters split evenly between the Reasoner (32B) and Generator (32B) towers, is the flagship. It is a datacenter-grade model designed for maximum physics accuracy — the kind of model you use to generate synthetic training data at scale, validate control policies in high-fidelity simulation, or distill into smaller models for deployment. NVIDIA positions Super as the model for post-training and domain adaptation, where accuracy matters more than inference speed.

Cosmos 3 Nano, with 16 billion total parameters (8B per tower), is the workstation-class variant. It runs on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU and delivers high-quality video and action reasoning in fractions of a second — fast enough for iterative prototyping, real-time policy evaluation, and embedded research setups. For startups and academic labs without access to datacenter clusters, Nano is the entry point.

Cosmos 3 Edge, announced as "coming soon," targets real-time inference on edge devices like the Jetson AGX Thor. Its parameter count and architecture have not been disclosed, but the intent is clear: NVIDIA wants Cosmos 3 to run on the robot itself, not just in the cloud.

The benchmarks back up the claims. Cosmos 3 is ranked #1 among open models on Artificial Analysis, Physics-IQ, PAI-Bench, R-Bench, RoboLab, RoboArena, VANTAGE-Bench, and TAR. These are not narrow benchmarks — they span world generation accuracy, physics plausibility, action policy quality, and vision understanding. Across every category where open models compete, Cosmos 3 leads.

The Cosmos Coalition: Open Models Need Open Ecosystems

NVIDIA did not stop at releasing a model. It also announced the Cosmos Coalition, a global collaboration of world model builders committed to advancing open foundation models for Physical AI. The founding members tell an interesting story about where this technology is heading: Agile Robots, Black Forest Labs, Generalist, LTX, Runway, Skild AI, and Dyna Robotics.

Black Forest Labs and Runway are best known for their work in generative video and media — Flux.1 and Runway Gen-3, respectively. Their presence in a physical-world modeling coalition signals that the boundary between media generation and world simulation is blurring. A model that can generate a video of a robot picking up a cup and a model that can predict the physical outcome of that same action are, it turns out, not as different as the industry assumed. LTX brings expertise in real-time video generation; Generalist and Agile Robots bring the robotics deployment perspective; Skild AI brings embodied foundation models trained on diverse robot hardware.

The coalition is not a marketing gimmick. Members contribute models, research, and evaluation techniques. They gain access to Cosmos 3 technologies, training tools, and NVIDIA DGX Cloud infrastructure. The goal, according to NVIDIA, is to create a shared commons for world model research — analogous to what the LLaMA ecosystem did for language modeling, but for the physical world.

Who Is Building on Cosmos 3

Industry adoption was announced alongside the model. In robotics, Agile Robots, Doosan Robotics, LG Electronics, Samsung, and Skild AI are building on Cosmos 3 for perception, simulation, and policy training. In autonomous vehicles, Li Auto — one of China's largest EV makers — is using the model for scenario generation and closed-loop validation. In vision AI, Centific, Fogsphere, Linker Vision, Milestone Systems, and Yuan are integrating Cosmos 3 into their computer vision pipelines for physical-world applications ranging from surveillance to industrial inspection.

The breadth is notable. Cosmos 3 is not being adopted by a single vertical — it is being evaluated across robotics, automotive, security, and manufacturing simultaneously. That is unusual for a model that has been publicly available for less than a week. It suggests that NVIDIA has been seeding the model with lead partners for some time before the public release, and that the demand for open Physical AI foundation models was already high.

The COMPUTEX Context: Cosmos Is One Piece of a Much Bigger Puzzle

Cosmos 3 did not launch in isolation. Jensen Huang's GTC Taipei keynote was a cascade of announcements that, taken together, map NVIDIA's strategy for the physical world more completely than any single product release could.

Alongside Cosmos 3, NVIDIA launched the Isaac GR00T reference humanoid robot — a full-stack platform built on the Unitree H2 Plus chassis with Sharpa Wave dexterous hands and a Jetson AGX Thor T5000 brain. But the software infrastructure around it is what makes the hardware relevant. Isaac Lab 3.0 ships with the Newton physics engine developed in partnership with Disney Research and Google DeepMind, providing what NVIDIA claims is GPU-accelerated physics simulation with unprecedented fidelity for contact-rich manipulation. MuJoCo-Warp, part of the Newton ecosystem, achieves 70x speedup for humanoid motion training and up to 100x for complex hand manipulation — the kind of acceleration that turns weeks of training time into hours.

NVIDIA also previewed GR00T N1.7 and GR00T N2 — the next iterations of its generalist robot foundation model — alongside Alpamayo 2 Super, a reasoning model for autonomous vehicles built on the same architectural principles as Cosmos 3 but optimized for on-road perception and planning. The Alpamayo announcement confirms that NVIDIA views autonomous driving and robotics as two branches of the same Physical AI tree, sharing infrastructure, training pipelines, and increasingly, foundation models.

In a move that received less attention but may prove equally significant over time, NVIDIA announced that ABB, FANUC, YASKAWA, and KUKA — the four largest industrial robot manufacturers by installed base — are integrating Omniverse and Isaac simulation into their digital twin and robot programming workflows. Collectively, these companies represent over 2 million deployed industrial robots. Getting their simulation and programming pipelines onto NVIDIA's stack represents a beachhead in industrial automation that no competitor can easily dislodge.

Two new open datasets — SDG-PhyxSim and SDG-RobotSim — were released alongside Cosmos 3, providing pre-generated synthetic data for physics simulation and robot manipulation training. These datasets are designed to reduce the data generation burden on individual teams, accelerating the feedback loop between training and deployment.

What Makes This Different

The Physical AI landscape has not lacked for foundation model announcements. Google DeepMind has RT-3 and Gemini Robotics. Skild AI has its own embodied model. Tesla is training Optimus on proprietary data at unprecedented scale. Against this backdrop, what makes Cosmos 3 genuinely different is not its benchmark scores or its parameter count. It is the combination of openness and multimodality — and specifically, the inclusion of action trajectories as a first-class modality.

Previous open models for Physical AI have been either vision-language models that stop at perception, or video generators that stop at simulation. Cosmos 3 is the first open model that closes the loop: it perceives, it simulates, and it generates actions. That is not just a technical convenience. It means that a robotics team using Cosmos 3 can train a perception model, validate it in simulation, and distill a policy — all within the same architectural framework, without stitching together incompatible models from different research labs. The engineering overhead of keeping multiple models in sync, which has historically consumed a significant fraction of robotics R&D budgets, is eliminated at the architectural level.

The open release is equally consequential. Closed models, however capable, create dependencies that slow the field down — researchers cannot reproduce results, startups cannot fine-tune for niche tasks, and the community cannot collectively improve the model. By releasing Cosmos 3 under an open license, NVIDIA is betting that the ecosystem effects of openness will generate more value for its platform than exclusivity ever could. It is the Android strategy applied to Physical AI: give away the foundation model, sell the infrastructure (GPUs, DGX Cloud, Omniverse, Jetson) that everyone needs to run it at scale.

The Bottom Line

Cosmos 3 is not a finished product. It is a starting point — a foundation that the community will build on, fine-tune, critique, and improve. The model has limitations that will become apparent as more teams put it through real-world testing. The "omnimodel" framing invites scrutiny: can one architecture truly excel at all the modalities it claims to handle, or will specialized models continue to outperform it on individual tasks? The answer will shape not just Cosmos 3's trajectory, but the architecture of Physical AI itself.

What is already clear is that NVIDIA has crossed a threshold. With Cosmos 3, the company has moved from providing parts for Physical AI — GPUs for training, Omniverse for simulation, Jetson for deployment — to providing the cognitive architecture that ties them together. The "big bang" Jensen Huang described is not a prediction. It is an invitation. And the code is already on GitHub.
