The Robot That Works 22-Hour Shifts: Inside Eternal.ag's Bid to Automate the Greenhouse

Published June 7, 2026

In a greenhouse complex in Zeeland, in the southwest corner of the Netherlands, a robot is picking tomatoes. It has been doing this since early morning. It will continue past midnight, stopping only briefly for maintenance, before resuming at dawn. The robot, built by the German startup Eternal.ag, harvests truss tomatoes at a steady rate of roughly 120 trusses per hour, using computer vision to assess ripeness, cutting each stem with surgical precision, and depositing the fruit into collection bins without ever touching it by hand. No human operator supervises it. No joystick jockey sits in a control room. The robot is fully autonomous, start to finish.

The product is called Harvester, and it represents something the global greenhouse industry has been waiting for: a commercially viable, fully autonomous harvesting robot that does not require structural changes to existing greenhouses and works reliably enough that growers are willing to pay for it. In an industry where labor availability has declined by roughly 30 percent across Europe since 2010, and where the average greenhouse worker is getting older every year, the arrival of a robot that can work 22-hour shifts without complaint is not a novelty. It is a necessity.

From Cologne to the Dutch Horticulture Belt

Eternal.ag was founded in 2025 by Renji John and Sherry Kunjachan, two engineers who met in the German robotics ecosystem and identified what they describe as a structural mismatch in the greenhouse industry. European greenhouses are among the most technologically sophisticated agricultural facilities in the world, equipped with automated climate control, irrigation systems, and logistics networks. Yet the actual harvesting of crops still depends overwhelmingly on manual labor. And that labor pool is shrinking.

The company is headquartered in Cologne, Germany, with a second office in Bengaluru, India, where its 26-person team spans two continents. The structure is deliberate: the hardware and systems engineering happens in Europe, close to the customer base, while the software and AI talent draws from India's deep pool of engineering graduates. This cross-border setup is increasingly common among European robotics startups that need to manage costs while staying close to their markets.

The team's decision to target greenhouse tomatoes rather than field crops or other greenhouse vegetables was strategic. Tomatoes are a high-value crop with year-round demand. Greenhouse tomatoes in particular are grown in controlled environments that are more predictable than open fields, making them a feasible entry point for autonomous harvesting technology. Truss tomatoes, the specific variety the Harvester targets, grow in easily identifiable clusters and have a relatively unambiguous ripeness signal in their color transition. This makes the computer vision problem simpler than, say, distinguishing a ripe strawberry from an unripe one under variable lighting conditions. The strategy is classic robotics: solve a well-defined problem in a constrained environment first, then expand the capability set as the technology matures.

The Harvester: What It Actually Does

The Harvester is a mobile robotic platform that navigates independently through commercial greenhouse rows. It uses AI-powered computer vision to assess each tomato truss on multiple criteria: ripeness level, accessibility, stem orientation, and whether the fruit is already damaged or diseased. When it identifies a truss ready for harvest, a precision cutting mechanism severs the stem at a specific angle designed to minimize damage to the plant and optimize post-harvest shelf life. The harvested truss is then placed into a collection container, and the robot moves on to the next target.

Key performance numbers are as follows. The robot operates up to 22 hours per day, with the remaining two hours reserved for charging, cleaning, and software updates. Harvest speed is approximately 120 trusses per hour in steady-state operation. The robot requires no structural modifications to the greenhouse; it navigates using the existing aisle layout and can switch rows autonomously. Integrated safety systems detect human workers and pause operation when someone approaches within a predefined perimeter. The system is designed for continuous improvement: every harvesting action feeds data back into the AI model, refining picking accuracy and decision-making over time.

The most technically distinctive aspect of Eternal.ag's approach is not the hardware itself but how it is trained. The company uses a simulation-first development methodology. When a potential customer expresses interest, Eternal.ag asks for the greenhouse blueprints. Within roughly one hour, the company can rebuild that entire greenhouse inside its simulation environment, creating a digital twin with accurate row spacing, lighting conditions, and even floor friction characteristics. The robot is then trained for thousands of virtual operational hours in this simulated environment before any hardware is deployed. By the time the physical robot arrives at the greenhouse, the AI models have already logged extensive experience in that specific facility. Renji John describes the effect: "When the robot first arrives at a greenhouse, it is like an 18-year-old that already knows how to drive. Within a couple of weeks, it is already like a 40-year-old." This approach compresses what would normally be months of on-site calibration into days, dramatically reducing the cost and risk of deployment for both Eternal.ag and its customers.

The Crisis That Is Driving Adoption

The greenhouse industry across Europe and North America is facing a structural labor shortage that is not cyclical but permanent. The European Union's horticulture sector has seen labor availability decline by approximately 30 percent since 2010. The workforce is aging: the average age of agricultural workers in the Netherlands, one of the world's most advanced greenhouse economies, has been steadily rising, and younger workers are not entering the profession at replacement rates. Seasonal migration, long the backbone of European greenhouse labor, has become less reliable due to shifting immigration policies and improving economic conditions in Eastern European source countries.

The result is a market where labor is not just expensive but increasingly unavailable at any price during peak harvest periods. Greenhouse operators have invested heavily in automation for climate control, irrigation, and logistics, but harvesting has remained stubbornly manual because it requires the combination of visual assessment, delicate manipulation, and adaptive decision-making that robots have historically struggled with. The arrival of reliable AI-powered computer vision and dexterous manipulation has changed this calculation. Eternal.ag is part of a wave of agricultural robotics startups betting that the technology has finally crossed the threshold from lab-grade to field-grade reliability.

June 2026: A Month of Milestones

June 2026 has been a defining month for Eternal.ag, with three developments that collectively signal the company is moving from pilot phase to commercial scaling.

First, the company expanded its deployment with its first customer, Van Noord Growers, in Zeeland, Netherlands. After a pilot that began in September 2025 with a single Harvester unit on 5.5 hectares of tomatoes and cucumbers, the grower has committed to scaling to three robots across 8.5 hectares by mid-summer 2026. Van Noord Growers is a midsize family operation, not a corporate mega-farm, which makes the expansion decision significant: it suggests the Harvester's return on investment is already calculable at a scale that is relevant to the broader industry, not just deep-pocketed multinationals. The robots operate alongside existing workforce, handling the high-volume repetitive harvesting work that is hardest to staff, while human workers focus on quality inspection, plant maintenance, and other higher-value tasks.

Second, Eternal.ag announced a collaboration with Rijk Zwaan, one of the world's leading vegetable breeding companies. The partnership is one of the more interesting developments in agricultural robotics because it addresses the problem from the other direction. Instead of only making robots better at harvesting existing crop varieties, Eternal.ag and Rijk Zwaan are exploring what tomato traits would make plants inherently easier for robots to harvest. This includes characteristics such as improved fruit accessibility, optimized plant architecture, and growth patterns that reduce the variability a robot must handle. The collaboration is a recognition that the most efficient path to automated harvesting may not be building smarter robots, but breeding plants that are easier for robots to handle. Michiel Zwaan, Crop Manager for Berries and Tomato at Rijk Zwaan, said: "Robotics will play an important role in the future of automatic greenhouses. That's why working together is important. This collaboration gives us the chance to try things out in practice." The Harvester will be demonstrated at Rijk Zwaan's Trial Center High-Tech in De Lier during GreenTech Amsterdam week in June 2026.

Third, the company appointed Roel Janssen as Chief Business Development Officer. Janssen, a veteran of Philips Lighting, Signify, and vertical farming operator Planet Farms, brings deep connections in European horticulture and a mandate to drive expansion into North America. The appointment signals that Eternal.ag is preparing for a transatlantic push, likely within the next 12 to 18 months.

The Trolley: A Bet on Adoption Paths

One of the more quietly strategic moves Eternal.ag has made is the launch of an omni-directional electric Trolley that can be upgraded into a full Harvester robot. The Trolley is a simpler, cheaper product that provides immediate labor savings in greenhouse logistics moving harvested crops, transporting supplies, and assisting with plant care. Crucially, 100 percent of the Trolley purchase price is credited toward a future Harvester upgrade. The logic is straightforward: get the robot into the greenhouse in a simpler form, let the grower build operational confidence and see the ROI, then upgrade to full harvesting capability when ready. It is a lower-friction entry point that reduces the perceived risk of adopting autonomous technology in an industry where upfront capital expenditure decisions are scrutinized carefully.

This approach suggests that John and Kunjachan understand something not every robotics founder grasps: the hardest part of selling robots to traditional industries is not building the technology but overcoming the buyer's fear that the technology will not work as promised. A Trolley that demonstrably works today is a stronger sales argument than a Harvester that might work tomorrow.

Funding and Market Context

Eternal.ag has raised 8 million euros to date, from investors including Simon Capital, Oyster Bay Venture Capital, EquityPitcher Ventures, and Backbone Ventures. The round is modest by the standards of the humanoid robotics sector, where hundreds of millions of dollars flow each quarter, but it reflects a different reality: agricultural robotics does not require the same capital intensity as general-purpose humanoids, and the path to revenue is shorter when you are selling to customers who have an immediate, quantifiable problem.

The global agricultural robotics market was valued at approximately $12.6 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $28.2 billion by 2030, growing at a compound annual rate of roughly 22 percent, according to The Business Research Company. The broader autonomous farm equipment market, which includes autonomous tractors and drones, is even larger, projected at $113 billion by 2030. Eternal.ag is entering a field that is crowded with startups but dominated by no single player, and where the unmet need dwarfs the current supply of working solutions.

Competition and Differentiation

Eternal.ag is not alone in pursuing greenhouse automation. Companies like Metomotion, Dogtooth Technologies, and Harvest Automation have developed robotic solutions for various fruit-picking tasks, while larger agricultural equipment manufacturers have been slowly adding autonomy to their product lines. What distinguishes Eternal.ag is its combination of full autonomy (no human in the loop), its simulation-first development methodology, and its willingness to work with breeders on crop genetics. The partnership with Rijk Zwaan is particularly difficult for competitors to replicate because it requires deep integration between robotics engineering and plant science two fields that rarely collaborate closely.

The company's thesis that partial automation human-in-the-loop or teleoperation does not solve the structural labor problem is also a meaningful differentiator. Renji John has stated this position bluntly: "If you put a human in the loop, you are basically pushing the problem away." In a tight labor market, a robot that requires a skilled operator is not a solution to the labor crisis. It is a different kind of problem. Eternal.ag's insistence on full autonomy whether it proves out operationally will determine whether the company scales or stalls.

What Comes Next

Eternal.ag's roadmap includes expanding to additional crops cucumbers, strawberries, sweet peppers, and eggplants are all under evaluation and scaling its deployment footprint across Europe and into North America. The company has stated an ambitious long-term vision: fully automated greenhouses by 2040, where all routine crop work is performed by robots and human labor is reserved for higher-value activities such as strategic planning, crop science, and system management. Whether that timeline is realistic depends on factors that are largely outside the company's control: the pace of AI advancement, the availability of capital for agricultural automation, and the willingness of greenhouse operators to trust their crops to machines.

For now, the robot in Zeeland is picking tomatoes, 22 hours a day, seven days a week. It is not a research prototype. It is not a pilot project with an uncertain future. It is a commercial product doing a job that a human being would otherwise have to do. In an industry where labor is the binding constraint on growth, that is a more significant milestone than it might sound.
